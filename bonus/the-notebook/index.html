Paper Graves / Bonus scene

THE NOTEBOOK

Silas's first day.

Dark romance for adult readers, 18 or older. Read the content warnings before you start.

The scope was cut for a rifle. I put it on a funeral.

Second level of a parking garage two blocks off, my knees on concrete that had held the night's rain and was handing the cold back up through denim. A seam in the deck above me let go into a puddle by my boot, one drop, then a long wait, then the next. Across the street and down, on a square of lawn, people in dark coats stood around a table with an urn on it. They had burned her before her sister got to see her. Beside the urn, a photograph somebody had cropped out of a happier picture.

I did not keep the glass on the photograph. A photograph is a thing you can set down.

The woman at the rim of the group wore that face alive.

Black dress. Hair down, dark, sliding across her left eye whenever she tipped her head, and she never once pushed it back. She stood a half step outside the others, a guest nobody had asked. I held the glass on her face waiting for it to give. It did not give. Her left hand kept going to her right wrist, to a scar there. The thumbnail worked at its edge, small, like a woman easing up the corner of a piece of tape.

My right hand would not close around the focus ring. The knuckles had gone the color of a plum. The swelling ran into the wrist, and the bones under it had not gone back where they belonged. A hospital would have asked how. I turned the ring with my left and braced the scope on my forearm like a man who had never been taught better. Nobody on that lawn looked up at the concrete.

In my jacket pocket, Lena's chain. Gold, thin as thread, knotted from the night it came off her. Working the knots out would take a minute. I had not spent it and I would not. My thumb found them through the lining and stayed.

I had an account ready if anyone asked, and nobody there knew to ask. I went over it once more, word for word, while a man in a collar said her name wrong.

The file on the sister was thin. Art professor at the university north of the city. Painted in oils. Left-handed. Sister of Lena Castellano, deceased, cause of death on record accidental overdose.

I had opened it the week before. The photograph clipped inside caught me sideways, and I shut the file and opened it again to be sure of what I saw. Lena's sister. For the people who had done that to Lena, that was enough. It was the whole of why I was on my knees in a parking garage with a scope on a woman who did not know my name.

That was the job. I said so, in the garage, to the puddle.

Others cried. She stood through the whole of it with her hands still and her jaw set and her thumbnail at the scar. When it ended she was the first to leave. She did not run.

Rain came back with the dark, days later.

I sat in a rented Civic on Elm with the windows sweating and the rain ticking the roof. The street ran black and wet under a lamp that put a varnish on the whole block. Her building had a buzzer panel by the door with tape over one of the buttons. Third floor. Her window was dark when I parked.

Wet wool off my own coat. A pine tree on a string turning off the mirror. The notebook in my jacket pressed its wire spiral into my ribs. Black cover, three to a pack, my initials and the date inside the cover written in the rental lot and nothing under them.

Her window lit up.

Kitchen first. Overhead light, hard and white, her shape crossing it twice. I put the glass on her. She filled a kettle and stood at the counter with her back to the window while it came up. Then a filter over a cup, and the water poured in slow, in a spiral. She opened the refrigerator and took out a carton and a spoon and measured one level spoonful into the cup and stirred it and put the carton back. She looked at the cup a while before she picked it up.

Black on the days she taught. Cream on weekends, one spoon, and never a second one. I know that now. That night I did not know it was a rule. I watched a woman measure cream into a cup, her sister not a week gone, and the rain kept on and I did not look away.

The living room lamp came on, lower, gold. There was a gap in her curtains she had not fixed, a hand's width, and through it the end of a couch and her. Rain ran the windshield and softened her to a shape and cleared and softened her again. She sat with her left knee drawn up under her and a book in her right hand and the thumbnail of her left between her teeth. She read. She turned a page and turned it back. The cup sat on the table beside her and I watched the steam come off it and thin and quit, and she did not pick it up again.

Lena would have been on the phone by then. Lena would have had music going loud enough for the street, and a bottle open, and somebody over, her whole body thrown at the night to keep the night off her. This woman sat under a lamp with a book she was not reading.

She put the book face down. She set both palms flat on the table in front of her and held them there. Her shoulders did not move. Nothing in the room moved. Then she took her hands off the wood and carried the cold cup to the sink and I lost her behind the wall. My own hand was flat on the dash. I had no memory of putting it there.

The bedroom window went gold, then dark. Rain on the roof. The rental's clock said past one, and I sat in the car until the lamp had been out long enough that a man with a job would have driven off.

The notebook stayed in my jacket. It was for the job.

The first entry went in on a Monday.

0647: Subject leaves apartment. Black coat. Coffee from Stanton's, black, large. Route: Elm to 4th to campus. Arrived 0712. No deviations.

Long wool, the coat. The coffee came out of Stanton's in a paper cup she did not drink from until she was in the car. She drove the long way in. There was a shortcut at Pines she did not take. Pines and 4th has no left turn signal, I worked out later, and she would not bet her clock on it.

She parked in the garage on the second level, west side, and took the stairs down. The elevator stood open and lit at the end of the row. She walked past it with the cup in her hand and her boot heels loud on the concrete, and the stairwell door banged shut behind her. Maybe an elevator is a box with one door. Maybe it was slow. I wrote the stairs and left the maybe out.

0830: Subject enters Whitaker Hall, Room 214. Lecture: Introduction to Drawing. Seventeen students present. Subject engages. Animated. Confident in material. Departed 0952. No contacts of concern.

I had a bench outside and a newspaper open on my knee. Through the east window she stood at the front of the room with charcoal worked into her sleeve, pointing at a thing on a table. A room full of nineteen year olds looked where she pointed. Her hands stayed quiet. Lena talked with her whole body. This one talked with a piece of charcoal and a jaw.

Subject. The pen bore down on the S and raised it on the back of the page.

The notebook filled. Gas on the same weekday. Groceries at Rosetti's at the end of the week, early, with the misters still hissing over the greens. The same bag out every time. Sourdough, yogurt, eggs, black beans, whichever fruit was cheapest, and the dark bottle of olive oil that cost twice what anything else in the bag did. Two lemons, never more, paid for in cash. Portfolios graded at the coffee shop on Marshall on a schedule a stranger could set a watch by.

A student who stayed after class and stood too close to her desk, whose name went in and did not come out. I wrote her coat down every morning for a week before I quit writing the coat. Gold studs in her ears on Tuesdays and Thursdays that I thought I knew from another ear and could not swear to. A call from a number she did not like that put her pacing the apartment with the phone held out from her body like a thing that had bitten her.

I took a room across the street. The curtain gap did its work. She read before bed with the thumbnail between her teeth and the knee drawn up. A third cup went cold on the table every night, and some pages she read twice and never noticed.

The word held. Subject. I wrote it at the head of every entry and it held for a while and then it did not hold. The entries kept coming anyway, in a hand that had started pressing harder.

I said the account to her once. In my head, in the room across the street, with her lamp out and the rain going. I said it the way I would say it if she ever asked. I listened to how it sounded, and I fixed the parts that did not sound right, and I said it again.

Then other people started coming down her hallway.

Tonight her car is on the second level, west side, and I am standing between it and the wall where the stairwell light does not reach.

The dose is in my pocket, measured to her weight. There is a house in Montana with the sheets on the bed tucked tight at the corners and a toothbrush still in its wrapper standing in a cup. There is a road most maps do not carry. The notebook rides my jacket with a page waiting. I have not decided what to call her on it.

Boots on the stairs. The door opens. The elevator stands lit and empty at the end of the row, and she walks past it.
