ROOFTOP

Cassian’s first night.

They gave me a folder and a fee and a form that wanted her priced by the month. None of the three survived her. I did not know that yet, that first night on the roof, the tar still holding the day’s heat through the soles of my boots.

Ashford Monitoring handed me the work the way it handed me all of it, a folder slid across a table in a room rented by the hour and a fee that would clear in three parts on a schedule no bank would flag. No name on either side of that table a court could keep. In the life before the roofs I had counted their coin a long time, sat in a back room and kept the far end of the same business, a column in a ledger for what a body came to once the buyer was done with it. A coin in my palm meant inventory and nothing more back then, a number and no face over it. The man training me called them receipts. I had run a lot of those columns and never once had to look at the face at the top of one, not until they put a scope in my hands and a folder on my knee and told me to set the number myself. I know now what those columns were pricing. That first night I let the new folder mean what the old ones had meant, paperwork with a fee at the end of it, and I carried it up the stairs light.

The folder was thin. That was the insult of it. A woman reduced to nine pages: date of birth, a nursing pin she had not quite finished earning, a fourth-floor walk-up with a lock I could have turned with intention and a hairpin, a shift pattern at a hospital that had not yet learned to be afraid of its own parking structure. A next of kin line with a mother’s name, a date of death beside it, and no one else under it at all. A photograph clipped inside the cover, the kind taken from a car with a long lens, her face turned three-quarters away and blurred at the jaw where she had moved. One line beneath it in the small deliberate hand those people used when they wanted a thing to sound like commerce. Assess for suitability. File monthly.

Suitability. I knew the word the way a surgeon knows the word margin. I know now what it was really pricing. That night I let it mean paperwork, a box to fill: is she reliable, is she alone, does her file close clean and stay closed. What the number at the bottom bought, and who did the buying, I did not carry up onto the roof. I was new enough to the work to keep it out there in the dark past the glass, and the story I fed myself was that I did not need to know.

I found the roof before I found her. A professional checks the roofs first. The building across from hers offered four candidate lines, and I walked all four in the last of the daylight. Three of them looked into her flat and left me lit from behind while they did it, a shape any sleepless neighbor could mark and keep. The fourth gave a line without giving one back. A squat mechanical housing on the northwest corner of the structure across the street, a steel box maybe four feet to a side, with a lip of shadow along its north face where a man could sit and not stand against the sky. From behind it I had her bedroom window square in the glass, her kitchen window beside it, her fire escape, and the mouth of the alley she used as a shortcut she should not have used.

I mapped my way off that roof before I mapped anything of hers. Two ways down, the service stair on the east wall and a fixed ladder on the south face, and a third if I was willing to hang and drop the last eight feet to the loading dock. I checked all three before I let myself look at her window a second time. Doors first. People second. That is the order that keeps a man breathing in this work, and I had kept breathing a long time.

Then I claimed the perch, before I let myself wonder why I wanted it kept. Before the climb I had stopped at the deli on the corner and bought a coffee, black, in a paper cup with a lid that never seats flush. I set it on the gravel by my boot. Steam came up off the split in the lid and stood a moment in the cold before the dark took it. I left it to cool. The housing broke the wind coming up the avenue and threw a wedge of shadow I could sit inside without becoming a shape against the sky. I set my back to the cold steel, ran the sightline to her windows once more to be sure of it, and settled in for a long night that was only work. No one came up to take the perch from me. Not that night. Not any night after.

The first hour, she was only data. Lights on at 6:40. The blue wash of a laptop thrown up against the ceiling. A shape crossing the window, economical, a woman who had learned to move through a small space without lighting more of it than she needed. She sat down at the kitchen table with a heavy textbook open flat, its spine cracked white, and a highlighter she kept capping and uncapping against her lip. She worked. Every few minutes she pressed the heel of her hand into one eye the way a person does at the tail of a double shift. The pin was not on her yet. It would be, when she finished the program. I wrote the time down.

She got up once for water and crossed the room without waking the overhead, found the glass by feel at the drying rack, filled it, drank it at the sink with her back to me, and sat back down. A person who lived alone and knew the exact geometry of her own dark. Ninety seconds, table to sink to table. I logged the trip.

The folder had given me a dead mother and given me nothing about a locket. The locket was at her throat regardless. A small gold thing on a chain gone pale where it had ridden against skin for years, since long before nursing school, since some year on the far side of the date printed beside her mother’s name. She bent into the lamp to read and the gold caught the light and threw one hard point of it back at me through the scope, and then she turned a page and it was gone. I wrote that down too, in the column for things that were true and useless. The plan was to watch her the way I watched all of them and hand her back a yes, a no, a number.

By the coffee, the steam had gone thin. A thread of it now where an hour ago there had been a plume. I did not reach for it.

Then an ambulance turned onto her street and lit the fronts of the buildings red, and she went to her window instead of away from it.

That is the thing they never put in the folder. What a person does with a siren. Most people close the blind; the city teaches you young that the red light is somebody else’s worst night arriving and you do not owe it your face. She lifted the sash instead. She leaned out into the cold over a four-story fall she did not seem to weigh, forearms on the sill, and she watched two medics work a body on the pavement below her.

I put the scope on the pavement long enough to log it. A man down outside the corner store, a spill of groceries around him, a can rolled to the curb. One medic driving the heels of both hands into his chest, arms straight, rocking his whole weight down through them. The other fitting a bag over the man’s mouth and squeezing it in time. I know the rhythm. Everyone in my line knows the rhythm; it is the one thing everybody else is trying to keep going and we are sometimes there to end.

Then I put the scope back on her, because she was the job, and found her mouth moving.

Counting. I understood it after a moment. She was counting the compressions under her breath, four floors up and no use to anyone, the way you hum a song you cannot stop, her hands not down there on that chest and wanting to be. She was twenty years old, at an open window she had no idea a soul could see, spending herself on a stranger below her for no reason and no audience she knew of.

My hand had stopped on the scope. I turned the focus a hair past where the job needed it, closer, until her mouth filled the glass and I could read the count straight off her lips, one and two and three and four, and I held her there, nearer than a professional ever lets himself stand to the thing he is meant to be measuring, and I did not pull back. It was the first thing I took from her that was not in the folder. It would not be the last.

Down on the pavement one medic sat back on his heels. The other checked his watch and said a word, and the first one nodded and let his hands go still on the dead man’s chest. She saw it. Her lips quit halfway through a number. She did not close the window even then. She stayed with her arms folded on the sill and her breath going up white while they zipped the shape into the bag and lifted it and rolled it into the back of the truck, and she kept a kind of watch over a man who would never learn that anyone upstairs had counted for him at all.

I reached for the coffee without taking my eye from her. It had gone cold in the cup, a skin drawn tight over the top of it, the steam an hour into the air and gone. I set it back down on the gravel by my boot. I did not drink it. Not that night. Not after.

Her lamp did not go out. I stayed, and the street beneath me emptied car by car and window by window, until her window was the last one lit on the block and I was the one thing across from it still awake.

The bathroom light came on near two, amber behind the transom over her shower. It fogged, held, then went dark. The bedroom lamp took up the slack. She kept it low, as she always would, a lamp on a dial turned down and never off, so the burn of it made her window a frame instead of a mirror. I had walked those light angles myself that evening, when I found the perch, and I knew where the streetlamp died before it climbed to the fourth floor and left her lit from inside and me unlit across from her. She lived in a box with the light on. I sat in the dark and read the box.

Pattern of life. That was the word for it, the word I meant to set on the form when the night was logged. Subject bathes late off a long shift. Lamp low. An entry in a column, a thing with a number beside it and nothing else. She was a data point. I was a man doing clean work. The scope was a tool in my hands. I had run a lot of columns and I told myself this was one more, and the pen stayed capped on the form on my knee.

She crossed the room in an oversized tee, white, the hem at mid-thigh, a towel in one hand and her hair down and damp against her shoulders. Barefoot on a floor I would never hear. She worked the towel through her hair at the dresser, unhurried, and the hem rode up one thigh with the reach of her arm, and I held the scope on the window and not on the thigh. The pattern and not the body in it. I wrote nothing down.

She turned the lamp lower. The white of it went gold. The shadows sat down into the corners and the glass gave up the last of its shine, and there was only the room and the woman in it, closer in the scope than the job had any use for.

She lay back on the bed. The window was uncovered, as it had stayed uncovered all night, as she would never once cover it. The bed sat at an angle to the glass and gave me the partial line: her left side, the rise of her hip, the bend of her knee, her right hand at rest on her stomach above the hem. Her left hand went behind her head. She looked at the ceiling. I logged the time out of habit, the pen still capped, the number kept only in my head.

Her right hand moved. Down. Under the hem. Slow, the kind of slow a person only manages alone, with nowhere to be and no one to answer to. Her knees parted, not far. Her wrist changed its work, not the angle but the pace of it, a small circle now, deliberate, and the cotton drew tight between her thighs.

The form had a paragraph for this and I knew it by heart, because I had written it. The assessor keeps the distance the subject is owed. I had set that line down myself in a room rented by the hour, and it held for about one breath.

Her knees opened wider. Her fingers worked under the cotton, two of them, index and middle, the heel of her hand pressed where the fabric had gone dark. I turned the focus wheel. A hair closer. The same theft as hours before, when the ambulance lit the street red and I had turned it past the job to read her count off her lips, one and two and three and four. The same motion of my thumb. The same crossing. The glass sharpened and I could see the wet drawn dark and spreading in the cotton, the shift of her fingers under it.

For one breath my head put me in the room. At the foot of the bed. The gold light on my hands. I cut it the way you cut a thought in the field, fast, before it can root. I was not in the room. I was on a roof in the cold with a scope and a form and a job that needed doing. She was there. I was the dark across from her. The glass was the glass.

I counted her the way I count everything. The rise of her ribs and the fall of them. The lift of her hips off the mattress, small at first, then less small. Her breath I could not hear, only read, in the hollow her stomach made on each pull of air. Her left hand came down from behind her head and took a fist of the sheet at her hip, and the knuckles went pale in the low gold.

I set the form and the capped pen off my knee and down onto the gravel, by the cold coffee. I opened my belt one-handed. My right hand stayed on the scope, the eyepiece hard to my socket, the focus locked where I had left it. My left hand found the buckle, the button, the zipper. The cold took my skin when I pulled myself free. I had been hard since the hem rode up her thigh. My knees came down onto the tar, the grit printing through my pants, and I stayed on them.

A professional makes no sound on a roof. I said it to myself. I said it again, and I kept it.

Her rhythm built. I matched it. My hand closed around my cock and I held my pace to hers and I watched through the glass and the gold. Her head turned on the pillow. Toward the window. Toward the black gap in the roofline where I knelt, where no light reached and nothing stood against the sky. Her mouth opened. I read the shape of it, the soft O of it, the catch in the line of her throat when her breath stopped and started and stopped again. She was looking at the dark. Not at me. There was no me to look at. I was an empty place on an empty roof, and she gave it her open mouth and did not know she gave it anything at all.

Her back arched. Her shoulders came off the mattress and her fist pulled the sheet into a knot and her knees pressed in around her working hand. Her throat moved. Her stomach clenched hard under the thin cotton and her free hand shook where it gripped the sheet, the small bones standing up along the back of it. She held. One count. Two.

My silent count broke. The discipline that held my pace went out of me and my hand moved fast and rough and my jaw locked shut and I did not breathe and I made no sound. Two beats after her, I spilled onto the tar, hot against the cold of it, and my hand kept on and my eyes stayed open and my right hand stayed on the scope, and I watched her come down while I came.

She went soft. The arch left her back. Her hips settled and her knees fell open and her hand came out from under the hem and rested, glistening, on her stomach. Her mouth closed. Her head rolled back to center. She breathed, slow, the limbs gone heavy. The tee had ridden up above her hips and she left it. Her underwear had pulled dark and twisted to one side and she left that too. She lay in the gold and the after of it and she looked like a thing I had no right in the world to see. A thing taken.

I was on my knees on a roof. The tar was cold. What I had spilled on it was already cooling in the air. My cock was in my hand and my hand would not stop shaking and I could not make it stop. My heart worked too hard, harder than the work I had just done, the blood ahead of the head the way it always runs. The scope was warm from my eye. I did not look away and I did not put myself away.

The contract had been the thing happening on this roof when I climbed up. Somewhere between the focus wheel and the tar under my knees it stopped being that, and I could not name the beat. You know it after, and after is on the far side of the line the wheel does not cross back. She was a private woman, an adult, alone in a private room, and the uncovered glass and the low lamp were not an invitation to anyone. They were a life. I had taken from it, and there was no column on any form they gave me for what I was now. The shame of it did not go anywhere. It changed in my hands into a decision. I would watch her. Not the folder’s watch, the one that ends when the fee clears. Mine. I would keep the window and the door and the street and the long dark, for as long as she needed keeping, and I would start the count with the man on the roof.

She reached for the lamp. For half a breath her eyes went to the dark roofline across the street, to me, through me, finding nothing there to find, and then the light went out. I stayed exactly where I was. The word waiting on the form, the one word the whole night came down to, stopped being a word I knew how to spell.

Her window was a black square among the other black squares now. I fixed my clothes and sat back off my knees, my spine to the cold housing, and I took the form up off the gravel and the capped pen with it and set them on my knee. For eleven minutes my hand did not move.

I have been precise about everything else I have ever done. That gap is the one entry in the ledger I have never closed. Eleven minutes, the pen dry, the small box printed beside the word waiting on its mark, and my body still holding the last hour, the heat of it not out of me yet. The word would not sit still long enough to mean a thing. Suitable. Suitable to whom, and for what, and in the hand of a man who an hour before had knelt on the tar of a roof she would never stand on and taken off her a thing she had handed no one. By every measure those men cared about she was perfect. Alone. Missed by nobody with the money to come looking. I could not make the pen touch the paper.

I closed the folder.

The strategy came later. Once I understood what the folder had really been asking, I needed a reason for what I had already done that first night, and I built one the only way I build anything, in a column. One: a rushed assessment is a bad one. Two: a bad one gets a woman lifted who should not be. Three: a botched lift leaves a crater, and a crater was the single thing the men who paid me feared. Four: I was, before anything else, a professional protecting a margin. Four clean reasons, entered in order, and not one of them was why I closed the folder that night, before I knew enough to have a reason at all. I have been telling them to myself for eight years anyway.

What I did next was not strategy. Over the weeks that followed I built her client file clean and complete and fit to be read by a man who meant her harm. Then I built a second one underneath it that no client would ever see. The flat competent line her mouth made when a pressure dropped, the same set to her face for a dying man and for a jammed pump. That had no column on any form they ever gave me. I gave it one. The nine seconds it took her to set a line in a drunk who would not hold still, timed off my own watch, because I timed everything and had stopped being able to not time her. The eight bars she hummed against the highlighter cap, always the same and never the words. The night she walked home through the rain without once checking over her shoulder, because no one had taught her yet that she had to.

Watching had a shape when I started and it was losing that shape. A yes, a no, a number was the whole of it once. Now I logged the color of her scrubs and told myself it was pattern of life. I knew what it was turning into. There was nowhere I could keep it honestly but that second file, in a hand only I would ever read, so I kept it there, and I turned each page of it over once and set it down where it belonged.

The reports went in monthly. That was the cadence the form wanted, a fresh page on her every thirty days with a number at the bottom. The first one I filed said diminished market interest, and I wrote it before I understood the half of what interest meant or who was doing the wanting, only that I needed the number going down. Month four I wrote subject relocated, no fixed asset value change, a lie with a real street behind it. Fourteen months of write-downs stacked up in that file, her worth sliding a little further into the floor each page. Twice the client asked for updated photographs. Twice I sent him prints that showed a woman and told him nothing, a scarf, a bad angle, a face turned from the lens in the same three-quarter blur as the first photograph in the folder.

I built the case out of true things arranged to lie. A coworker too attentive. A regular at the diner who would notice if she stopped coming in. A brother upstate, invented, with a temper and a lawyer, invented too, the kind of loose thread the Gallery hated worse than grief. On paper she turned into a woman who would leave a crater, page by monthly page, until even a patient buyer would count her more trouble than she was worth. I am good at my work. Ashford believed me. They had never once been given a reason not to.

When the file had gone that quiet, I put it to sleep for good. I did not write the last verdict. I stamped it. The block was red rubber worn smooth at one corner from the years of hands before mine, and I rolled it once across the pad and set it square to the page and pressed it home with my thumb, one hard press, hard enough to feel the grain of the desk come up through the paper. ASSET UNSUITABLE. Red block letters, heavier along the left edge where my thumb had borne down, a little starved of ink across the top of the second S. Two words in the only language the form knew how to read.

The fee had cleared long before. The client carried his money off, I told myself, to another city and another name, and I made certain I never learned either one.

And I stayed.

The contract was finished. No fee, no folder, no client left who cared what the roof across from her building held. There was only a man who had spent eleven minutes not writing and would not get them back, and who had decided that whatever came for her would have to come through the dark she could not see into first.

Year on year I renewed a contract no one had offered me. The dormant file still wanted feeding, quarterly now instead of monthly, so I fed it. Four dull pages a year, each one saying less than the last, because a file no one bothers to read is a file that keeps her invisible, and invisible was the only safe thing I could still make her. I wrote the newest one a few weeks ago. She finished the program and pinned the little enameled bar to her chest, and I set the date down with the rest of it. One hard winter she learned to check over her shoulder, and I hated the man who taught her that lesson and was glad down to the bone that she had learned it. When a man let himself into her building with a key that was not his, I broke his hand in four places on the landing one floor below hers and left him to find his own way down in the dark. Nothing a report would carry. No face anyone would keep. Then the roof again, and I logged the night ordinary, because by then it was. Hundreds of ordinary nights, a woman reading late and a man across the street writing down that she was safe.

I missed one thing that first night. The Gallery keeps its own books.

They do not forget an asset they let walk. They only take their time. One day a man who never quite needed the file to remember her pulls it anyway, eight years cold, runs his thumb down a column, and stops on two words stamped in my hand, and he does the arithmetic I spent eight years betting no one would sit down and do. He finds the coworker moved away years back. The regular dead. The brother upstate a name with nobody attached to it. He finds a woman still alone, worth more to them now than she was at twenty, a yes I had filed as a no.

Then he goes looking for the man who lied to him. And the man who lied to him is on a roof on the northwest corner, exactly where he has been for eight years, having taught her to watch every direction but the one it was coming from.

I did not know, that first night, tar warm through my boots and her mouth moving in the cold, whose thumb would come to rest on that column.

I know now.
