Chapter One

Trauma Bay 2. The overheads caught me short of the vending machine, cup still in my fist, still full. My braid sat tight to my skull, pinned flat for every shift, because loose hair and an open wound have no business sharing a room. Nine hours left on the clock. The job had already started.

Dispatch gave us four minutes and a pressure of eighty over palp, which in practice meant less than four minutes and probably not even that. The rapid transfuser stood primed, lines flushed, before the ambulance bay doors banged open, O-neg already running warm through the tubing, a red that stays just red to anybody who’s never stood where I was standing. My hands had finished the math my head hadn’t caught up to, tubing checked twice by feel, a reflex older than whatever thought used to drive it.

“Male, twenty-eight, single GSW right lower quadrant, no exit,” the medic said, breath already spent, already handing him across the gap from his gurney to ours. “Pressure’s been sliding the whole ride.” Twenty-eight. Near enough to mine that it snagged someplace I had no spare second to visit, so onto the shelf it went with everything else that wasn’t useful yet.

“Trauma two, we’re up.” Dani had the monitor leads on him before my scissors finished his shirt, one long stroke through cotton from collar to hem. His belly met my palm rigid on the right side and the wrong color, skin stretched drum-tight over blood with nowhere left to go, still warm under my fingers in spite of what the color claimed.

“Sixty over nothing,” Dani called. “He’s crashing.”

“Wide open.” I ran the transfuser to max and watched the bag collapse into him at a rate that would’ve hurt a man who was awake. He wasn’t. His eyes had settled at half-mast, the place bodies drift to when they start deciding things the mind no longer gets asked about. The plastic went cold and slack against my palm as it drained.

The surgeon came in already scrubbing, already calling for the OR, and for six minutes the room held nothing but hands and numbers and the flat working hush of people too busy to be scared. My breath ran shallow behind the damp mask. Slowing it would have cost seconds the patient needed worse than I did. A phone chirped from a coat on the hook by the door, three tinny bars of a song nobody had the time to resent. I called his pressure every thirty seconds. Dani called his sats in the level voice she never spent anywhere but here. His fingers had gone the pale pink of a fish’s underside, cool tipping into cold, his skin telling the story ahead of any monitor, and I closed both my hands around his wrist less to hunt a pulse the screen already gave me than because a hand that cold wanted another hand on it, machine or no. When the OR team took him through the double doors he read ninety over sixty and climbing, and that was as close to good news as this job gives out before dawn. I took one whole breath before I let the number go.

I peeled my gloves off inside out, dropped them in the red bin, and let my shoulders drop the two inches they’d climbed without telling me.

“You good?” Dani was already stripping her own gloves, already reaching for the coffee she kept warm on the burner at the nurses’ station, in open violation of every fire code St. Ambrose kept on its books.

“I’m always good.” Which wasn’t a lie, exactly. I worked two fingers under the collar of my scrubs and found the locket warm against my sternum, thumbed the clasp open and shut against the spring, a habit I’d kept since I was nine and the thing had belonged to my mother instead of me. It didn’t mean anything. A habit, like cracking knuckles, like clocking every doorway before looking at the crowd. I let it fall. It settled back against my skin and warmed itself by degrees.

After that the board emptied on its own schedule, one gap breeding two, then four, the waiting room winnowed down to the walking wounded and the genuinely bored. I did a lap. That was the job too, the piece no recruiting poster admits to, the lap between traumas: palpating the stitched-up, eyeballing the splinted, and the man in bay six who’d spent three hours certain his heartburn was a heart attack and was, technically, right that something in his chest was trying to kill him. Just not tonight.

A woman in bay four wanted her IV out before the antibiotics finished dripping, and I talked her down to twenty more minutes on numbers instead of feelings, numbers being the thing that talks anybody down: half a bag remaining, call it fifteen drops a minute, sit and watch the chamber with me if you don’t trust the pump. She watched the chamber. Her fingers had bleached white around the bed rail, and I watched them give, one knuckle at a time, while the drops kept their unhurried count. Somewhere behind a curtain a kid cried in the particular rhythm of a child angrier than hurt, and under it a mother’s voice ran low and singsong, patient enough that I admired her without saying a word of it.

That was when he registered. Older man, seventies, parked in the family waiting area with a woman I took for his daughter, and his hand on the armrest carried a fine fast tremor, up and down, four beats a second if it was one. The evenness of it walked me two steps closer before I’d decided to walk anywhere.

“He came in for his hip,” the daughter said once I’d crouched by his chair. “But his hand’s done that since this morning. The other ER said maybe a stroke starting.”

I gave it another few seconds, and the rhythm kept ticking in my head after I looked away. Fine, fast, metronomic. Lithium toxicity climbed the differential on its own, higher still with a new diuretic in the picture, and it didn’t clear stroke.

“Any medication changes lately? A new water pill, maybe, for swelling?”

“He started one last month. For his ankles.”

“Is he still on his lithium?”

“How did you know he takes lithium?”

“Pattern and medication history. We still need the rest of his neuro exam.”

At the chair I ran him through smile, speech, gaze, grip, arm drift, then carried the onset time, the med change, and every finding down the hall to the attending. She repeated the neurologic exam on the spot and had him shifted into a monitored bay, new leads, new numbers on the screen. No focal deficit, no other acute stroke sign, so she skipped the stroke alert; she ordered a lithium level, a metabolic panel, renal studies, and told us to escalate any change immediately.

Forty minutes later, a little after two, the lithium came back toxic and the kidneys came back badly off baseline. The attending went through the numbers against her repeat exam and took the plan over from there. The daughter cried with relief instead of grief, and I stood at the curtain’s edge a beat longer than the job asked, watching her hold her father’s shaking hand like the shaking had just gotten easy to hold.

“Marlowe’s a witch,” the charge nurse said, mostly to her pen, already committing it to the chart. “Board’s dead anyway. Get out of here before I change my mind.”

I wasn’t going to argue with a gift. Dani caught me at the lockers, phone up, screen glow on her chin, already recording for the podcast she swore would make her famous one day, this story about a nurse three counties over who’d disappeared her own patient’s chart along with the patient. “You’re leaving me,” she said, eyes still down. “With Bay Six and his heart-attack heartburn.”

“He likes you better anyway.”

“Everybody likes me better. It’s not a competition I’m losing gracefully.” She clicked the phone dark and looked at me properly, which happened maybe twice a shift, actually looked instead of talked past. “You good? You went quiet on the lithium guy.”

“I’m always good,” I said again, because it beat explaining that quiet was just what thinking looked like on me. She let most of it go and bumped her shoulder into mine on her way past toward the supply closet.

I clocked out at 2:47, hung my badge under my coat by the lanyard, hands running on long habit, and went out through the ambulance bay into an October wind that bit, in a city that hadn’t earned a break from summer yet. Cold got into the gap at my collar first, then my wrists where the sleeves rode up, and I hunched into the crouch winters teach, shoulders up, chin down, a posture that cost nothing and paid out every time.

Home ran twenty minutes if I kept moving, and at three in the morning I always kept moving. Moving targets are boring targets, and boring was what I aimed for at that hour. Streetlights every half block, most of them doing their jobs.

The river kept to my left the whole way, mostly smell and barely shape in the dark, silt, diesel, and under both of them something colder, climbing into my collar no matter which way I turned it. My breath came out white and hung there longer than it had any right to before the wind took it. An empty bus rolled past, lit up like an aquarium, nobody home behind any of that glass. Somewhere back toward the warehouses a dog built itself into a frenzy and quit mid-note, like a hand had come down on it. For half a block my own footsteps rang too loud, same as they always did after a shift, my ears still tuned to a trauma bay’s particular hush and not yet recalibrated for an empty street.

Halfway past the warehouses I thought I caught a second set of footsteps falling in under mine, off by half a beat, an echo bouncing off loading-dock steel, maybe, or nothing at all. I stopped to retie a boot that didn’t need it and listened hard enough to hear my pulse at work in my ears. Nothing. No footsteps. No shape peeling off a doorway. The river, doing what the river always did. I called it fatigue, seven hours on my feet and a GSW that had run my own pressure up along with the patient’s, and I walked on at the pace I’d started with instead of the faster one my legs wanted, because outrun a feeling and all you teach it is that it worked.

The Meridian sat two blocks south of my building, window booth burning yellow and full even at this hour, one shape bent low over a coffee cup. I left them their coffee. Out front the neon buzzed through the two letters that had never worked right, MER DIAN, and the gap had quit registering with me; anything broken long enough turns into part of the scenery.

My building squeezed in between a laundromat and a dry cleaner shuttered so long the windows wore paper, four stories of brick that had been red once and had settled into the color of a bruise. The laundromat hummed through the party wall. Cold hurried my knees, so I took the front steps two at a time, habit doing the driving. At the top step I turned once, full circle, streetlight, doorway shadow, parked car, empty stoop, sweeping the block like I’d sweep a waiting room before reaching for a door. Four years of nights installs that in a person without asking permission. Nothing read wrong. Four flights up to 4C, seventy-two steps I could have climbed blind, and some mornings coming off a double, near enough had.

A scarf lay folded across the top of my stair rail. My key, already seated in the lock groove by feel, stopped where it was.

I gave it a long look. Gray wool. Dani’s present, last Christmas, because gray, she’d said, gave me an excuse to skip thinking about what color to wear. I’d worn it out yesterday morning. Tonight I’d left without it, and I’d have sworn to that, because at the bus stop I’d patted my coat pocket, figured I’d dropped it somewhere between the dry cleaner’s and home, and figured I didn’t have time to care. My pulse jumped the gun before the thought finished, taking its head start like it does on a coding patient, out ahead of the monitor’s scream.

It’d been placed. Folded in thirds down its length, edges squared, flat across the top rail, tidy as a dish towel laid out on a table for somebody to find. The gutter wind found the loose tail hanging below, and the fold above it didn’t move.

Up the stairwell, empty. Down the block, streetlight to streetlight, nothing moving but a plastic bag turning slow circles in the wind. Whoever set it there had stood where I was standing. Had to have. Four flights, and they’d measured the one spot my eye couldn’t come up without landing on. I know that trick. I use it myself, choosing the vein before the needle cap ever comes off. My breathing filled the concrete stairwell, bounced off it, and loud was warning enough.

I picked it up. Dry. That meant something, because the front steps had held last night’s rain until well past noon. Wool, and under the wool something faint enough that I might have invented it, night air and clean, soap maybe, nothing I could name. Anything crossing my hands got pinned down eventually, tremor or pressure or a lie told smooth in a waiting room. This wouldn’t pin. My own scarf, found and folded like it rated respect, left exactly where I’d have to see it and nowhere that said who.

I turned it over once, the half-turn I give a dressing when I’m hunting for blood coming through from underneath. Nothing on it but itself. No note. No pin. Just the fold, three even panels, corners squared like a bed made by somebody taught hospital corners once and never unlearned them. I knew that fold. I put it in sheets myself, tucking them under men too sick to manage their own, the fold that tells a patient somebody here has got you now, rest. Somebody had aimed that fold at me. My thumb found the crease and pressed it flat, like smoothing it could undo whoever had put it there.

Threats spoke a grammar I read on sight: a slashed strap, a word keyed into paint, a thing taken instead of handed back. This was somebody finding what I’d lost, carrying it up four flights, setting it down clean and square, and the whole message fit inside that. They could, they had, and they wanted me to know it took hands, not luck.

I checked my phone. The screen glared bright and useless in the dark stairwell. No missed calls. Nothing from Dani about a button or a dress, nothing to explain a folded scarf turning up on a rail I’d passed a hundred nights while it hung there doing nothing but stay missing. I pocketed the phone and dragged cold air deep into my ribs, the way I’d coach a terrified patient. It worked on me about like it worked on them. Moved my feet. Did nothing for my heart.

A neighbor, then. Or the wind had done something strange and my memory stranger to match it. I unlocked the door faster than the chill alone could have made me move, threw the deadbolt behind me, threw the chain too, which most nights I didn’t bother with. I stood in my own dark kitchen with my coat still buttoned and the scarf still in both hands. The rattle of the chain against my knuckles, going into its track, still wouldn’t get out of my ears.

I brought the wool to my face and breathed in. Wound check, I told myself. That was all this was.

Wool. Night air. Something clean I couldn’t name, sitting on my skin like a second coat.

My other hand was at the locket on its chain before I sent it there, thumbing the clasp open and shut until the cold brass bit into my skin, a habit nineteen years older than the question of who had handled my things.
