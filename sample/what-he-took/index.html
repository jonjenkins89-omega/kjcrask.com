CHAPTER ONE

NOWHERE

MARA

Detergent on the sheets. Store brand, cheap and chemical, nothing like the lavender Lena used to steal from Target because she said life was too short for generic. I know all of it before my eyes open. Smooth cotton under my cheek. Firm mattress. A flat sheet pulled tight at the corners, tucked hospital-style.

I open them.

White ceiling, smooth as a fresh roller pass. No popcorn texture, none of the cracked plaster from my apartment in Portland. The sheen reads as new paint. One overhead fixture, plain brushed nickel, switched off. What light the room has comes through a window on my right, pale gray, early gray, day still undecided. The fog behind my eyes hasn’t cleared.

I’ve never seen this room before.

Sitting up rolls fog through my skull, slow weather. Copper on my tongue, sleep, and something else, a chemical residue parked at the back of my throat. Palms flat on the mattress until the tilt settles. Cold fingers. Bare feet. Yesterday’s clothes still on me, the black jeans, the gray sweater with the cuff fraying at the left wrist, but no boots. No jacket. No phone.

The room itself is small. I measure it anyway, pressure and proportion, the negative space around each object, the composition I’d frame in my head before my hand ever touched charcoal. Teacher’s curse, artist’s compulsion, call it what you want. Right now it’s the only thing holding me upright, because the moment the measuring stops, the screaming starts.

Not a hospital. Not a motel. Nowhere I’ve been.

Standing puts cool, solid floor under my feet. My knees feel wrong, loose, the joints packed with something softer than bone. I press a palm to the wall and the plaster gives back smooth and cool. Real walls. A real house. At the window, glass cold against my fingertips.

Trees. Pine and fir, thick and tall, crowded together up a slope that turns into a mountain. The sky behind them sits low and gray and blank. No power lines. No rooftops. No road from this angle. Trees, mountain, and a silence that doesn’t exist anywhere people live.

I try the sash. It gives, sliding up with a soft scrape of wood on wood, and cold air lands on my face. Pine, wet earth, distance. No screen. I could go through it. A short drop to dirt and pine needles, and nothing holds me back.

The window opens. I file that away.

Shut again. Turn. Cross to the door, plain wood, old brass knob, and wrap my hand around it.

The door swings open.

I stand in the hallway, bare feet on cool boards, and listen. The quiet has texture, and texture means occupied. An empty house goes silent all the way down, blood-in-your-ears silent. This one carries sound: a coffee maker clicking through its cycle somewhere, weight settling across a floorboard, air pushed aside just enough to give the stillness edges.

Somebody’s home.

My hands stay steady. I keep each breath slow and even and my fingers loose. Panic sits low in my gut, sour enough to gag me, but it isn’t driving this. Not yet.

A fresh toothbrush stands in its cup, packaging still sealed.

I back out and try the next door. A second bedroom, bigger than mine. Queen bed, made up, dark green quilt. Men’s boots on the floor beside it, twelves or thirteens, worn at the heel. A book face down on the nightstand, spine cracked; the title won’t come into focus from the doorway, and I’m not stepping inside. A flannel shirt hangs off the back of a wooden chair. Cedar, faint, and under it something unnamed, warm, animal, alive.

Past that, the living room.

This is no prison. I know the type: apartments with their windows blacked out, motel rooms where the carpet carried stains and the deadbolt sat on the hallway side. This isn’t that. This place is a home.

That’s what turns my stomach.

Through an open archway: the kitchen. Small, functional, clean. A gas range with black iron grates. The refrigerator hums in the corner; I open it and cold rolls over my wrists, eggs, butter, milk, vegetables in the crisper, a package of ground beef on the middle shelf. Pantry shelves hold canned goods, rice, pasta, flour, sugar, coffee. A cast iron skillet on the stove. A knife rack on the wall, and that rack keeps me standing there longer than it should.

I leave the knives where they hang. God, I want one; my fingertips ache with it. But taking a knife means admitting I might need one, and admitting I might need one means admitting something about where I am, and I’m not ready for that yet. Still measuring. Still learning what these walls can tell me.

There’s a second door at the back of the kitchen. I try the knob; it turns loose in my hand, no catch, no bite. The door opens onto a small porch, steps down to a yard of scraped dirt, and the dirt quits where the trees begin. A woodshed leans at the edge of the clearing. Past the shed, nothing worth naming. Slope. Canopy. Sky.

I shut the back door and cross the living room to the front. Same answer; the lock gives without argument. I pull it open and the cold comes through my shirt and lands flat on my sternum, mountain cold, the kind that makes its home in shade and altitude and has no plans to leave, whatever the month claims.

The porch wraps halfway around the house. Boards gone gray with weather, cracked here and there, solid enough, giving a dry knock under my bare feet. Adirondack chairs sit spaced along it, wide-seated, low-backed, and a man occupies the nearest one.

He holds a mug. Coffee; the smell reaches me before I clear the doorway. His gaze stays where it was, on the trees or the road or the middle distance or nothing at all. His right hand curls around the ceramic. His left lies open on the armrest. The door opening moves nothing in him. No flinch, no turn of the head. His weight never shifts across the chair. He sits like a man who expected company, or like a man who’d sit that way whether anyone came or not, and neither reading comes cheaper than the other.

“There’s food inside,” he says.

His voice is low. Quiet is the wrong word for it. This is pitch, depth, sound built in a deep chest off thick cords, resonance parked at the bottom of the register, and it reaches my sternum before it reaches my ears. He might be remarking on clear skies. He might be announcing the pot’s on. In his mouth it’s an ordinary morning, me a guest under its roof, nothing about any of it asking to be explained.

I don’t answer. I read him instead, the old habit, breaking a subject into planes, sorting shadow from light, because that’s what my hands do when fear arrives and I can’t afford to stand in front of it. I measure.

He’s built on a scale the gym doesn’t manufacture. None of that inflated vanity muscle, shoulders pumped for mirrors. Functional mass, broad through chest and shoulder girdle, the frame that comes from hauling weight over distance, from axe work, from lumber, from whatever men carry in country where the nearest town survives mostly as a rumor. His henley is dark, sleeves rolled to mid-forearm, and the muscle beneath runs dense and roped, tendons surfacing whenever his fingers shift their grip on the mug. His hands are large. Scarred. White lines cross the knuckles of his left hand, thin and old, legible from where I stand. Across the back of his right hand a burn scar pulls toward the wrist, that patch of skin glossy and drawn tight, a half-shade lighter than the rest of him.

His face is angles. Heavy jaw, heavier brow, a nose that took a bad break and healed a degree off true, drifting left. Dark hair cropped short, cut for use, nothing spent on style. Wind combs the treeline behind him; nothing on him moves. A scar rides the left side of his jaw, beginning just below the ear, ending at the corner of his chin. No surgeon drew that line. No accident put it there. Edge-work, a blade or something keener, held by a person who meant it.

Pinning his age defeats me. The face carries weather, the kind of aging that comes from seasons spent outside in hard country, logged in exposure rather than years. When his eyes finally come around to me they’re gray. Gray with nothing folded in, no blue, no green, flat and neutral as the sky pressing down on the ridge above us, and they settle on me with a pressure that holds no aggression anywhere in it. He’s assessing. Same work I’m doing.

My ribs cinch. Fear, but arriving sideways, dressed for labor. I take in his size, his stillness, the length of porch between us. Tall, heavy with muscle, seated like the house grew up around him and the mountains arranged themselves at his back. Me smaller, barefoot, planted in the middle of nowhere, studying proportion regardless: shoulder span against waist, light lying along his cheekbone, the hard charcoal shadow packed under his jaw, the softer grain of scar tissue at its border.

His throat bobs when he swallows his coffee, and I hear it go down, and I hate that I’ve filed it.

“Where am I?” The question leaves my mouth level. That part stuns me. Level exists nowhere else in my body. In there I’m a glass somebody set on a slanted shelf, waiting on gravity.

He looks at me. Those flat gray eyes, holding. Another drink, unhurried, then the mug meets the wide arm of the chair with a knock.

“Montana.”

One word. He spends it and goes back to his trees, and I stand in the doorway with cold pressing up through the boards into my bare feet, the house leaking warmth past my shoulders, and Montana hanging in the space between us, round and bright as a dropped coin.

Portland. I’m from Portland. Yesterday I was in Portland. In my apartment, I think, though the sequence has gone soft at the seams. Grading portfolios, or at the university, one or the other. I reach for the last thing I remember and the reach costs me, a thumb pressed to a bruise, the outline firm while the interior smears toward its edges. My car. I was in my car. The parking lot at the university. It was dark. I was tired. And then.

Nothing follows. The question sits in the air between us and the quiet answers for him, my own pulse loud in my ears, cold climbing past my ankles.

“How did I end up here?”

He lets it hang. Back at the tree line a bird fires off two notes, sharp, bright, interested in neither of us. My toes have started to ache down in their joints, the chill working inward through skin.

“You should eat something,” he says.

That’s no answer. I stand there and wait for a second sentence and a second sentence never comes. He has practiced this. His patience and mine are different animals entirely.

Mine is labor. Mine is the level voice I produce for a student whose portfolio has collapsed, the pause before I dial my father back, the fixed set of my face whenever someone says Lena’s name. Mine costs me hourly. His is architecture. It went up with the rest of him, load-bearing, permanent. He could sit in that chair through a full turn of seasons and never once reach for words to fill the quiet.

No car in the driveway. I turn a slow circle, cold air sliding past my ears, and check again. No car anywhere. Firewood stacked against the siding. A steel trough that might gather rain. The woodshed I’d marked earlier from the back porch. That’s the entire inventory.

Nobody bolted me in. Leaving is simply off the table. The difference ought to bite harder than it does.

“What’s your name?”

His eyes cut to me again, a brief flick of gray, and it lands on my sternum like a palm laid flat there, pressure without contact, weight without a hand. My skin lifts in answer. Arms first. Then the back of my neck. I hate it, whatever the name for it is, and I refuse to give it one. Named things turn real, and I’m carrying every ounce of real I can hold.

“Silas,” he says.

Silas. I roll the word around my mouth. It sounds like a noise this house would make on its own, a board settling, a log shifting in the stove.

“Why am I here, Silas?”

He rises without the chair announcing it, fluid for a man his size, controlled, a big animal crossing its own ground with nowhere in particular to be.

He stops above me. From this range I can read the scar on his jaw, the skin puckered along the seam where it meets unmarked tissue. I can pick out single lashes around those gray eyes, dark, straight. Higher up there’s a second scar, a nick at the corner of his left eyebrow.

“Eat,” he says. “I’ll explain later.”

He moves past me into the house. His shoulder clears mine only because I flatten myself against the doorframe, and his heat travels by so close I take it in through my sweater, or I manufacture it, and from where I stand the two versions are identical.

I stay on the porch. The cold turns serious now, actual teeth in it, my feet going numb on the weathered boards, my fingers curled shut inside my sleeves. The dirt road runs at the trees and quits there. Low gray sky pressed down on the mountains ringing this valley, close as walls, and my eye does the work it was trained for, composition, negative space, the rule of thirds, and files its report: nowhere to go.

Last time I wore this exact flavor of trapped, I sat in a records office in Eugene while a woman with kind eyes and a clipboard slid a sealed file across the desk. Acute combined drug toxicity, she said. The heroin and fentanyl in my sister’s system were consistent with accidental overdose. The remains had been cremated before release. I nodded. I signed forms for an urn I never believed in, and the whole time my body stood somewhere else, outside the building, the lot, the city, inside a room shaped exactly like this one, doors standing open, no exits anywhere.

Lena. Almost a year now. Grief wakes when I wake and pads after me from room to room. Some mornings I surface into a breathless blank where none of it exists yet. Then it lands. That brief mercy is the cruelest thing I own.

The cold finally shoves me indoors. I wrap my arms around myself and go in.

He’s in the kitchen. Eggshells crack against a bowl’s rim; butter hisses in a pan; warmth reaches the hallway and touches my face as I pass the archway without looking in. I take the corridor to the room I woke up in and pull the door shut. There’s a lock on my side, a plain bolt, hardware off any bedroom door in any house in the world. I slide it home.

A small sound. A final one.

I sit on the bed and drag the sheet around my shoulders, still cold to the bone, and my hands betray me, a fine tremor living out at the fingertips. I press them flat against my thighs and run the old exercise, in and out, in and out, the drill a grief counselor handed me before I stopped going, because she kept calling it a process and I kept wanting to call it a canyon, a hole in the ground where Lena used to be.

Nothing moves in this room. Even the sheet against my shoulders has quit its shivering.

What I’ve got: Montana. A house up in the mountains and a man in it called Silas, hands laced with old scars, eyes gone gray, and a patience that frightens me more than violence ever would. He left my door unlocked. The knives stayed in the kitchen. Windows that open, doors unlocked, and he told me to eat, and he’s in there right now breaking eggs into butter like the pan is the only thing under this roof that needs him, and I’m somebody he knows.

What I don’t have: any of the rest. Butter and eggs and coffee slide under the door, kitchen smells, ordinary as Tuesday, and my stomach knots. I’m hungry. Actually hungry. Sitting on a bare mattress with clean sheets around my shoulders, mouth watering over another person’s breakfast, and underneath it all the scar on his jaw, how it took the gray light on the porch, how I’d build that shadow in charcoal.

Charcoal. Never pencil. Pencil can’t manage the blur where tissue softens at the edges. You’d layer it slow, press harder where the scar cuts deepest, lift the stick clear of the raised ridge of healed skin. His face runs on contrast, bone doing its hard geometry, damage doing its soft ruin. I’d give a whole page to that jaw alone.

I fold forward until my forehead rests on my knees and shut my eyes. No screaming. Just the portrait building itself behind my lids, charcoal again, the scar, the hard seam of his mouth, gray light riding along his jaw.

Time goes somewhere without me. The white in the window warms by degrees, and then footsteps in the hall.

Heavy steps, even ones. A big man crossing his own floor, boards taking his weight with small groans, every step landing where he put it. He passes my door at the same pace. No slowing. No stopping.

He leaves the handle alone.

I track him down the hall and into the living room, and then the front door opens and shuts and the house settles into a particular quiet, the kind that means nobody else is in it. My hands press harder into my thighs. Breathe.

Violence I could work with. Threats I could name, measure, answer. If he’d stood over me when I woke, if a lock had turned, if the knives had vanished, I’d know which story I was sitting in. I’ve read that story. I’ve watched documentaries about it. Captivity has a language and I speak it. Control has a grammar and I know it cold.

This is some other thing. No grammar for it. No framework. I drag the sheet tighter. Firm mattress under me, pillow smelling of store-brand detergent, and past the glass the trees stand in their ranks, straight as soldiers, watchful as sentries, and if those trunks are bars, the cage they build is wide enough that the thing inside it hasn’t found the wire yet.

Somewhere in the house a clock I’ve never seen lets off a dry little tick. It carries through the walls, through the silence, through the thin wooden door and its sorry little lock.

Eyes shut, I go to Lena.

I don’t sleep.
